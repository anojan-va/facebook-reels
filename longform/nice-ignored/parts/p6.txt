So what do you actually do? Because the answer is definitely not to become a jerk.

The first thing is to learn to say no. Not rudely. Just honestly. "I can't this weekend." "I'd rather not." "That doesn't work for me." You don't need a long explanation. A clear no makes your yes worth something again.

I'll never forget the first time I said no to my boss. He asked me to take on another project, the third one that month, and I said, "I can't do that well right now with what's already on my plate." My heart was pounding. I thought I'd get fired. Instead, he nodded and said, "Okay, that's fair." And two months later, a coworker I'll call Mark, who'd been saying yes to everything just like me, was still drowning in extra work. I was the one who got asked to lead the next team.

The second thing is to stop apologising for existing. Watch how many times you say sorry in a day. Sorry for asking. Sorry for being late by one minute. Sorry for having an opinion. Every unnecessary sorry tells people you see yourself as a burden.

The third thing is to let people be disappointed. This is the hardest one. As a nice person, you feel responsible for everyone's feelings. But other people's disappointment isn't an emergency. It's just a feeling. They'll survive it, and so will you.

And the fourth thing is to give from fullness, not from fear. Help people because you want to, not because you're scared they'll leave if you don't. When your kindness is a choice, not a payment, people feel the difference straight away. And here's a test I still use. Before you say yes to something, ask yourself: would I still do this if they never found out it was me? If the answer is yes, that's kindness. If the answer is no, you're probably trying to buy their approval.
