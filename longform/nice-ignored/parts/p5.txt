The third thing is about edges. Nice people often have no shape.

When you're scared of upsetting anyone, you hide your opinions. You say "I don't mind" when someone asks where to eat. You laugh at jokes you don't find funny. You agree with whoever spoke last. And slowly, you become a little bit invisible.

People connect with people, not with mirrors. They need something to hold on to. An opinion. A taste. A strong feeling about pineapple on pizza. Something that tells them who you actually are. The next time someone asks where you want to eat, pick a place. Out loud. It sounds tiny, but it's a muscle, and every time you use it, you become a little more visible.

There's a well-known idea in psychology from Susan Fiske and her colleagues. When we meet someone, we judge them on two things: warmth and competence. Are they on my side? And are they capable? Nice people usually score high on warmth. But if there's no strength behind it, no confidence, no opinions, people don't feel respect. They feel something closer to pity. They like you. They just don't think about you.

And that was exactly my problem with Chloe. She liked me. She trusted me. But I'd never shown her anything that made her feel a spark. No opinions, no challenge, no edges. Just agreement. The guy who forgot her birthday at least felt like a real person with a life of his own.
