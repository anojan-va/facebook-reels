What if, tonight, the Moon simply disappeared?
No explosion. No warning. One moment it's there, and the next, the sky is empty.
It sounds harmless. The Moon is just a light in the sky, right?
But that rock has been quietly holding our world together for billions of years. It moves our oceans, steadies our planet, sets the clock for countless animals, and it even shaped the length of your day.
In this video, we'll follow what happens step by step, from the first night without it, to the strange, wobbling world it would leave behind.
And by the end, you'll see why some scientists think we might not even be here without it.
Chapter one. The first night.
It's a clear night, and the Moon is full. Then, in an instant, it's gone.
The first thing you notice is the dark. A full Moon lights the ground around a hundred times brighter than starlight alone.
Now every night is as dark as a new Moon night, forever.
Astronomers would actually love it. With no moonlight washing out the sky, the Milky Way would shine on every clear night of the year.
But something else changes right away, and you can't see it. The Moon's gravity is gone.
Earth and the Moon don't simply travel around the Sun side by side. They swing around a shared balance point, about four thousand seven hundred kilometres from Earth's centre.
Without the Moon, that dance stops, and Earth settles into a slightly different path around the Sun. The change is tiny, and you wouldn't feel a thing.
There's no earthquake, no flood, no sudden end of the world.
But at the next high tide, the ocean doesn't come as far up the beach as it should.
And that's where the trouble begins.
Chapter two. The tides fall.
Twice a day, all around the world, the oceans rise and fall. Most of that is the Moon.
Its gravity pulls on the side of Earth facing it a little harder than on the far side, stretching our oceans into two bulges. As Earth spins through them, we get high tide and low tide.
The Sun makes tides too, but they're less than half as strong.
So without the Moon, only the Sun's tides are left. They'd be less than half the size we're used to, and they'd look almost the same every day of the month.
No more spring tides. No more king tides.
In Canada's Bay of Fundy, home of the highest tides on Earth, the water can rise and fall by about sixteen metres. That show would shrink to a fraction of its size.
Tidal power plants, like La Rance in France, which has been making electricity from the tides since nineteen sixty six, would lose most of their energy.
The bigger change happens underwater.
Tides don't just move water up and down a beach. As they slosh across the ocean floor, they help stir the deep sea, mixing heat and nutrients through the oceans.
Weaker tides mean a calmer, more layered ocean, and over time, that could shift ocean currents and the climates that depend on them.
And along every coastline, a whole world is about to shrink.
The intertidal zone, the strip of shore that floods and drains every day, is home to crabs, mussels, sea stars, barnacles and countless birds that feed there at low tide.
With smaller tides, that strip gets narrower, and much of the life squeezed into it loses its home.
But for some animals, losing the Moon means losing something even more important.
Their calendar.
Chapter three. The animals that lose their clock.
Every year, a few nights after a full Moon in late spring, the Great Barrier Reef does something incredible.
More than a hundred kinds of coral release their eggs and sperm at almost the same moment, filling the water like an underwater snowstorm.
Corals have no eyes, but they can sense moonlight. Scientists think the changing light of the Moon helps them pick the right night.
Without the Moon, that signal disappears. Spawning could drift out of sync, and when corals spawn at different times, far fewer eggs meet.
On the beaches of California, little silver fish called grunion wait for the highest tides after a full or new Moon. Then they ride the waves up onto the sand to lay their eggs.
About ten days later, the next big tides wash the hatchlings out to sea.
With tiny tides that barely change, that perfect timing is gone.
In Delaware Bay, horseshoe crabs, animals older than the dinosaurs, crawl ashore by the hundreds of thousands to spawn on the high tides of late spring, with the biggest crowds around new and full Moons.
Their eggs feed migrating birds like the red knot, which flies thousands of kilometres and stops here to refuel.
Break the crabs' timing, and you break the birds' journey too.
And it's not just the ocean.
Dung beetles in Africa use polarised moonlight to roll their dung balls in a straight line at night.
Predators follow the Moon, too. Lions in Tanzania catch more prey on the darkest nights, when there's little moonlight.
So for lions, a moonless world might be a better world. For the animals they hunt, it's a nightmare.
Even tiny animals in the Arctic Ocean, during the months-long polar night, move up and down through the water following the Moon.
Take the Moon away, and one of the oldest clocks on Earth stops ticking.
But the biggest change of all would take much longer to arrive.
Our seasons.
Chapter four. Wobbling seasons.
Earth is tilted. Right now, its axis leans by about twenty three and a half degrees, and that tilt is the reason we have seasons.
When your half of the planet leans toward the Sun, it's summer. When it leans away, it's winter.
That tilt isn't perfectly fixed. Today it rocks gently between about twenty two and twenty four and a half degrees, over a cycle of about forty one thousand years.
Part of the reason it stays so steady is the Moon. Its gravity tugs on Earth's bulging middle and keeps our planet spinning like a steady top.
Without the Moon, the pulls from the other planets would have far more say over our tilt.
In the nineteen nineties, scientists ran simulations suggesting that a Moonless Earth could tip chaotically, anywhere from almost upright to more than eighty degrees.
Newer studies found the swings would probably be smaller and slower than feared, perhaps ten to twenty degrees, playing out over hundreds of millions of years.
But even that is enough to change everything.
Imagine Earth tilted at just ten degrees. Seasons would almost vanish. Summer and winter would feel nearly the same.
Now tilt it to forty five degrees. Summers would be scorching, winters brutal, and huge parts of the planet would spend months in darkness or in constant daylight.
Ice ages could come and go in new patterns, and whole ecosystems would have to move, adapt, or die out.
Mars is a warning. Its two tiny moons are far too small to steady it, and over millions of years its tilt has swung wildly, possibly beyond sixty degrees.
That's one reason Mars has gone through climate swings unlike anything we know.
But the Moon doesn't just steady how Earth leans. It also controls how fast it spins.
Chapter five. The length of a day.
Here's something strange. A day on Earth hasn't always been twenty four hours long.
As the tides drag against the ocean floor, they act like a very slow brake on Earth's spin. Every century, our day gets about two thousandths of a second longer.
It doesn't sound like much. But over hundreds of millions of years, it adds up.
When the dinosaurs lived, a day was about twenty three and a half hours, and a year had around three hundred and seventy days.
One study of ancient rocks found that one point four billion years ago, a day lasted only about eighteen and a half hours.
And that energy doesn't just disappear. As Earth slows down, the Moon gets pushed farther away. Every year, it drifts about three point eight centimetres further from us.
We know this precisely because Apollo astronauts left mirrors on the Moon, and scientists still bounce lasers off them today.
So what happens when the Moon is gone?
The biggest brake on Earth's spin disappears. The Sun's tides would still slow us down, but far more gently.
Our days would almost stop getting longer, and twenty four hours would stay twenty four hours for a very, very long time.
That sounds like good news. But it makes you wonder what kind of world we'd have if the Moon had never been there at all.
Some scientists think that without the Moon slowing it down, Earth could be spinning much faster today, with days far shorter than ours.
A faster spin could mean stronger winds, wilder storms, and a very different climate.
Which brings us to the biggest question of all.
Chapter six. A world without a Moon.
Think about all the things that would quietly disappear.
No more solar eclipses. The Moon is about four hundred times smaller than the Sun, but also about four hundred times closer, so it fits almost perfectly over it. Without it, the sky's greatest show is gone forever.
No more lunar eclipses. No more blood Moons.
Our calendars would lose their anchor too. The word month comes from Moon, and Ramadan, Easter, Passover, Diwali and Lunar New Year are all timed by its phases.
But some scientists think the Moon's biggest gift came long before any of that.
About four and a half billion years ago, a planet roughly the size of Mars is thought to have smashed into the young Earth. The debris came together to form the Moon.
Back then, the Moon was much closer, and its tides were enormous.
Some researchers think those tides, flooding and drying coastal pools again and again, could have helped the first building blocks of life come together.
Others think the Moon's steadying pull gave life billions of years of fairly stable seasons to evolve.
Nobody knows for sure. But it's possible that without the Moon, we wouldn't be here to miss it.
And here's a myth worth busting. Many people believe the Moon shields us from asteroids. It doesn't. It's far too small and too far away to block more than a tiny fraction of them.
So, what if the Moon disappeared?
The first night would be strangely beautiful, full of stars.
But slowly, the tides would shrink, the animals would lose their clock, and over millions of years, our seasons could start to wobble.
The good news? The Moon isn't going anywhere. It's drifting away by just a few centimetres a year.
In about six hundred million years, it will look too small to cover the Sun, and the last total solar eclipse on Earth will happen.
So next time you see the Moon, take a second look.
It's been holding our world together all along.
