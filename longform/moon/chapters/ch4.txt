Chapter four. Wobbling seasons.
Earth is tilted. Right now, its axis leans by about twenty three and a half degrees, and that tilt is the reason we have seasons.
When your half of the planet leans toward the Sun, it's summer. When it leans away, it's winter.
That tilt isn't perfectly fixed. Today it rocks gently between about twenty two and twenty four and a half degrees, over a cycle of about forty one thousand years.
Part of the reason it stays so steady is the Moon. Its gravity tugs on Earth's bulging middle and keeps our planet spinning like a steady top.
Without the Moon, the pulls from the other planets would have far more say over our tilt.
In the nineteen nineties, scientists ran simulations suggesting that a Moonless Earth could tip chaotically, anywhere from almost upright to more than eighty degrees.
Newer studies found the swings would probably be smaller and slower than feared, perhaps ten to twenty degrees, playing out over hundreds of millions of years.
But even that is enough to change everything.
Imagine Earth tilted at just ten degrees. Seasons would almost vanish. Summer and winter would feel nearly the same.
Now tilt it to forty five degrees. Summers would be scorching, winters brutal, and huge parts of the planet would spend months in darkness or in constant daylight.
Ice ages could come and go in new patterns, and whole ecosystems would have to move, adapt, or die out.
Mars is a warning. Its two tiny moons are far too small to steady it, and over millions of years its tilt has swung wildly, possibly beyond sixty degrees.
That's one reason Mars has gone through climate swings unlike anything we know.
But the Moon doesn't just steady how Earth leans. It also controls how fast it spins.
