Plus one year.
Look closely at any city. Thin green lines are cracking through the pavement.
Every winter, water seeps into those cracks, freezes, and expands, prying the concrete apart a little more. Every spring, plants move into the gaps.
With nobody mowing, lawns turn into meadows. Leaves pile up in gutters, block the drains, and the next heavy rain floods the streets.
City rats, which lived on our rubbish, start to go hungry. Without restaurants and bins, there is a lot less to eat, and their numbers crash.
Meanwhile, the sky is changing. With no factories and no traffic, the air gets cleaner surprisingly fast.
And at night, without street lights, the stars come back. For the first time in a century, you could see the Milky Way from the middle of a big city.
Up above, our satellites are still circling the Earth. Some will keep working for years. Then, one by one, they will slowly fall and burn up in the atmosphere. Others are so high that they'll keep orbiting for a very long time.
Plus ten years.
Wooden houses are in trouble. Roofs start to leak, the water rots the beams, and the first ones collapse.
We know how this goes, too. The abandoned city of Pripyat, near Chernobyl, was left empty in nineteen eighty six.
Today, trees grow out of its buildings, moss covers its floors, and its famous fairground is being swallowed by forest.
Back in our empty cities, fires are a growing danger. A single lightning strike can now burn through whole neighbourhoods, because there are no firefighters left to stop it.
And the ash makes the soil richer.
The forest is coming.
And it isn't alone. Oil refineries and chemical plants, with nobody to maintain them, slowly leak and corrode. Some of their tanks will rust through. For a while, the worst places will be toxic. But even there, life slowly finds a way.
