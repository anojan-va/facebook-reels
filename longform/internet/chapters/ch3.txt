Day two. Things get serious.
Overnight, the news finally reaches you, through the oldest social network in the world. Your neighbour, shouting over the fence.
Here's the problem. Almost everything you buy now travels on information before it travels on a truck.
Shops order food through computers. Warehouses track every box online. Trucks get their routes the same way.
And most supermarkets only keep a few days of stock on the shelves.
So by day two, the shelves start looking like the end of a party. Bread, gone. Water, gone. Toilet paper? You already know.
Delivery drivers sit in their vans with no addresses. Somewhere, your parcel is just driving around in circles, like a lost puppy.
At the petrol station, the card readers are down too. The queue for cash only fuel goes around the block.
The banks are open, but they can't do much. The cash machines outside are offline. And in some countries, like Sweden, cash is so rare that lots of shops don't even take it anymore.
At the airport, the planes can still fly. But booking, check in, and flight planning all run on networks. In July twenty twenty four, one bad software update grounded thousands of flights around the world. And the internet was still on that day.
Stock markets shut down. In twenty fifteen, a computer glitch froze the New York Stock Exchange for almost four hours. Now imagine a week.
But here's the good news. Your lights are still on.
Power grids run on their own control systems, separate from the public internet. So your fridge keeps humming, your taps still run, and you can still make toast.
You've never been so grateful for toast.
Still, by the end of day two, one thing is clear. This isn't a funny little outage anymore.
And tomorrow, you're going to have to do something you haven't done in years. Find your way somewhere. Alone. Without your phone telling you where to go.
