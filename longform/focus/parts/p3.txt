The second thing surprised me even more. You'd think the fix is simple. Turn notifications off, put the phone face down, done.

It isn't.

In 2017, a team led by Adrian Ward at the University of Texas ran an experiment with around eight hundred people. They gave them tests that needed real concentration. Some people had their phone on the desk, face down. Some had it in their bag or pocket. And some left it in another room.

The phones were silent. Nobody checked them. And still, the people with the phone in another room did noticeably better than the people with it on the desk.

Just having it close was enough to drain them.

Think about what that means. Part of your brain is always on guard duty. Is it buzzing? Did someone reply? Am I missing something? You're not using your phone, but you're still spending attention on not using it.

It's like trying to work with someone standing behind you, tapping your shoulder every few seconds. Except that someone lives in your pocket.

And it gets worse when you're stuck. The moment the work gets hard, that little voice in your head says, just check. And the phone is right there to say yes.

I tested this on myself. Same desk, same work. One week with the phone face down on the desk, one week with the phone in the kitchen. The second week, I finished more before lunch than I used to finish all day.
