Week one. Your new home.
Your house is called a habitat. Think of a tiny, very expensive tent that is trying very hard to keep you alive.
It holds the air you breathe, the water you drink, and the warmth that stops you from becoming a popsicle.
So where does the oxygen come from?
Mars has plenty of oxygen. It's just stuck inside carbon dioxide. So you split it.
NASA already tested this. A machine called MOXIE, about the size of a lunchbox, rode to Mars on the Perseverance rover and pulled oxygen out of the Martian air.
In total, it made about a hundred and twenty two grams. NASA said that's roughly what a small dog breathes in ten hours.
So the technology works. You just need a much bigger machine. Or a much smaller dog.
Next problem. Food.
You'll be growing some of it yourself, in a greenhouse with special lights. Lettuce, radishes, and yes, potatoes.
But you can't just plant them in Martian dirt. The soil contains chemicals called perchlorates, which are toxic to humans. You'd have to wash the soil first, or grow everything in water.
Farming on Mars is less "peaceful countryside" and more "chemistry exam you can eat."
And then there's the dust.
Martian dust is finer than flour, it's a little bit toxic, and it sticks to everything. Your suit. Your boots. Your hair. Your soul.
You will clean the airlock every single day, and you will still find red dust in places you didn't know you had.
And showers? Water is far too precious. You get a quick wipe with a wet cloth. Your crew will smell you before they see you. And you will smell them. Teamwork.
By the end of week one, you've learned three things. Breathing is a team effort. Potatoes are precious. And the dust always wins.
