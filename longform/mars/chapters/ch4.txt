Month three. The weird new normal.
By now you've noticed your days are a little longer.
A day on Mars is called a sol, and it lasts twenty four hours and thirty nine minutes.
That doesn't sound like much. But every single day, you get almost forty extra minutes. On Mars, you can finally hit snooze without feeling guilty.
At night, you look up and see not one moon, but two. Phobos and Deimos. They're tiny, potato shaped rocks.
And Phobos is so fast that it rises in the west, races across the sky, and sets in the east, about twice every sol. Even the moon here is in a hurry.
And it won't last forever. Phobos is slowly spiralling down toward Mars. In tens of millions of years, it will be ripped apart, and it may turn into a ring around the planet.
You try to call your mom. Bad idea.
Radio signals between Earth and Mars take anywhere from about three to twenty two minutes, one way.
So you say, "Hi mom!" and then you wait. And wait. Twenty minutes later, you hear, "Hi sweetie, can you hear me?"
Video calls are impossible. Texting is the only way. You are now basically sending letters, but faster.
And then there's something you can't see at all. Radiation.
Mars has no global magnetic field and a very thin atmosphere, so far more radiation reaches the ground than on Earth.
NASA's Curiosity rover measured it. Every day on the surface, you get roughly as much radiation as dozens of chest X rays.
That's why the smart move is to live underground, or to cover your habitat with a thick blanket of Martian soil.
Then one day, the sky turns brown.
A dust storm is coming. Some of them grow so big they cover the entire planet for weeks.
In twenty eighteen, one of those storms blocked out so much sunlight that NASA's Opportunity rover ran out of power, and it never woke up again.
