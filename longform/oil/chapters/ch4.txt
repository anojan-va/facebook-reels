Seven days without oil. And the cracks are everywhere.
The shelves are empty, and they're not filling back up.
Cities are the hardest hit. A big city can't feed itself. Its food comes from farms hundreds or even thousands of kilometres away.
Without trucks, fresh food rots in fields and warehouses, while people in cities go hungry. Governments start rationing, and the army uses its last vehicles that still move.
Rubbish starts piling up on the streets, because garbage trucks run on diesel too. After a few days, cities start to smell.
Fire brigades and ambulances share the few electric vehicles that exist. Doctors and nurses cycle to work.
Then we discover how much of our world is made of oil.
Plastic is made almost entirely from oil and gas. Packaging. Bottles. Phone cases. Car parts. And no new oil means no new plastic.
Hospitals feel it first. Syringes, gloves, IV bags, tubes. Most are plastic, and they're used once and thrown away.
Many medicines are made with chemicals that come from oil too. Even simple painkillers.
So hospitals start doing something they haven't done in decades. Washing and sterilising equipment, and using it again.
Around the world, roughly eighty percent of trade moves by sea. Now the ships just sit there. Phones, medicines, spare parts, all stuck in ports.
And some places go dark after all. Many small islands and remote towns make their electricity by burning diesel. For them, the lights finally go out.
One week in, the question isn't how to get to work anymore. It's how to get food to eight billion people.
And the answer, for now, is simple. We can't. Not all of them.
