The sun comes up on a very strange world.
Here's the first surprise. In most countries, the lights are still on.
Oil makes only a tiny slice of the world's electricity, around three percent. Most power comes from coal, gas, nuclear, water, wind and sun.
So your fridge still hums. Your phone still charges. And the internet still works. Everyone can watch the chaos live.
But try to go anywhere.
Petrol stations are empty. Buses are parked. Taxis are gone.
Schools close, because the school buses can't run. Offices empty out, because nobody can get there. Millions of people work from home, if they still have a job.
The new kings of the road are bicycles, electric scooters, and the tens of millions of electric cars on Earth. For the first time ever, the guy with the electric car is the most popular person in the neighbourhood.
Electric trains still run, and they're packed like never before.
At airports, the departure boards turn red. On a normal day, around one hundred thousand flights take off around the world. Today, almost none do.
And what about the emergency oil? Many countries store huge amounts for exactly this kind of crisis. The United States keeps its reserve in giant salt caves under the ground. But in our scenario, every drop vanished. Those caves are just empty holes now.
But the real problem isn't people. It's stuff.
Almost everything you buy reached you on a truck, a ship, or a plane. And more than nine tenths of the energy for all that transport comes from oil.
So on day one, the trucks that deliver food to supermarkets don't come. Most stores only keep a few days of food on the shelves.
And everyone knows it. So everyone goes shopping. At the same time.
Bread. Rice. Pasta. Canned food. Bottled water. Gone by lunchtime.
And people who live far from the shops have a new problem. The nearest supermarket used to be a ten minute drive. Now it's a three hour walk.
