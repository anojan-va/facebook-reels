The fifth reason is what you bring into the room emotionally.

Emotions are contagious. Psychologists call it emotional contagion. Without realising it, we copy the expressions, the tone and the mood of the people around us. So if every conversation with you starts with a complaint, the other person walks away carrying a little of that weight.

I'm not saying fake being happy. Real friends should be able to share the heavy stuff, and if you're going through a hard time, talk about it. But there's a difference between going through something and making negativity your personality. If people have to brace themselves before they call you, something needs to change.

I had a phase where every catch-up started with what was wrong with my job. One day a friend said, half joking, "So what's the disaster this week?" It was funny. It also hurt, because she was right.

Try this. Next time you meet someone, open with something good. Something small that made you laugh, something you're looking forward to. It changes the temperature of the whole conversation.

The sixth reason is about how conversations end, and almost nobody thinks about it.

The Nobel Prize-winning psychologist Daniel Kahneman described something called the peak-end rule. When we remember an experience, we don't remember every minute of it. We mostly remember the most intense moment, and how it ended.

That means the last few minutes of a conversation matter far more than you think. If you drag it out until there's nothing left to say, until you're both checking the time, that awkward fade is what they remember.

This is the one I changed first, and it worked almost immediately. I started leaving on a high. When we're laughing and the energy is good, that's when I say, "I have to go, but this was great." It feels rude at first. It isn't. It makes them want the next conversation.
