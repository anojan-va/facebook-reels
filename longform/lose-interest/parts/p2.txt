Let's start with the most common one. A sociologist called Charles Derber studied everyday conversations, at dinner tables, at work, between friends. He noticed that people constantly pull the conversation back to themselves. He called it conversational narcissism. And it's sneaky, because it doesn't look selfish. It looks like relating.

Your friend says, "I'm so tired, work has been crazy this week." And you say, "Tell me about it, my boss kept me until nine yesterday." You think you're connecting. You're showing you understand. But what you actually did was take the spotlight. Derber called that a shift response. The other option is a support response: "Crazy how? What happened?" That one keeps the spotlight on them.

When I first read about this, I recorded myself on a call with a friend, just to check. Then I counted. I pulled the conversation back to me eleven times in twenty minutes. Eleven. I wanted to disappear.

And there's a reason this hurts more than you'd think. In 2012, two Harvard neuroscientists, Diana Tamir and Jason Mitchell, scanned people's brains while they talked about themselves. The brain's reward system lit up, the same system that responds to food and money. People were even willing to give up real money just to keep talking about themselves. So when you take someone's turn to talk, you're not just being rude. You're taking away something their brain actually enjoys.

The fix sounds simple, but it takes practice. When someone tells you something, ask one more question before you say anything about yourself. Just one. Researchers at Harvard found that people who ask follow-up questions are rated as more likeable. Not the people with the best stories. The people who ask the second question.
