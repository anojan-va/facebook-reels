Chapter two. Impact.
The asteroid reaches the top of the atmosphere, a hundred kilometres up. At twenty kilometres every second, it punches through all of that air in about five seconds.
The air in front of it can't get out of the way. It gets squeezed so hard that it glows hotter than the surface of the Sun.
For anyone looking up, the sky splits open with a blinding white light.
Let's say it comes down exactly where the dinosaur killer did, in a shallow sea off the coast of Mexico.
This rock is so big that when its front edge touches the seabed, its back end is still about as high as a passenger jet flies.
To an object this size, the ocean is barely a puddle.
In less than a second, it releases about a hundred million megatons of energy. That's billions of times the bomb that destroyed Hiroshima, and more than ten thousand times every nuclear weapon on Earth put together.
The asteroid doesn't just make a hole. It vaporises. So does the rock underneath it.
For a moment, the hole is around thirty kilometres deep, more than three times the height of Mount Everest.
Then the walls collapse, and the floor rebounds upward, like a drop of water splashing back out of a pond. Within minutes, the crater settles into a scar about a hundred and eighty kilometres wide.
A column of molten rock and vapour blasts upward, right out of the atmosphere. Some of it reaches space.
The ground shakes with an earthquake far stronger than anything ever recorded. A blast wave races outward with winds faster than any hurricane. Everything within hundreds of kilometres is flattened, burned, or buried.
Further away, the ground jumps first. The sound of the impact arrives much later, as a roar louder than any thunder.
That's the first few minutes.
But the crater isn't the real danger. What comes back down is.
