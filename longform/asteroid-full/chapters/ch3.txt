Chapter three. The sky catches fire.
Remember the debris that was blasted into space? Gravity pulls it back. Billions of tonnes of it.
Over the next few hours, it rains down all over the planet as tiny glassy beads and burning rock. Every grain is a little shooting star.
Even on the far side of the planet, people look up and see the sky fill with meteors, as if every star is falling at once.
Trillions of them heat the upper atmosphere at the same time. Some scientists think the whole sky glowed like the inside of an oven, hot enough to set fire to forests on the ground below.
And we have evidence. Geologists have found soot and those same tiny glass beads in a thin layer of rock all around the world, laid down at the exact moment the dinosaurs disappeared.
Meanwhile, the ocean strikes back.
The impact shoves trillions of tonnes of water aside, and it comes rushing back in as a tsunami.
In twenty twenty two, scientists simulated the dinosaur impact and found the first wave may have been around one and a half kilometres tall.
In the open ocean, a tsunami can travel as fast as a jet airliner. Within two days, the waves had spread across most of the world's oceans, some still many metres high when they reached coastlines on the other side of the planet.
Now imagine that today.
Coastal cities around the Gulf of Mexico disappear under water within hours. Planes are grounded. Power grids fail. Satellites are hit by falling debris. For most of the world, the news arrives in fragments, if it arrives at all.
And yet, the fire and the water are not what wiped out the dinosaurs.
The real killer is quieter. It's the dark.
