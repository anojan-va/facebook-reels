Chapter four. The long night.
The impact threw something else into the sky. Dust, soot from the fires, and hundreds of billions of tonnes of sulfur from the rocks it vaporised.
High in the atmosphere, that sulfur turns into tiny droplets that reflect sunlight back into space. Within weeks, a veil wraps around the entire planet.
Midday starts to look like dusk. In some places, like night.
Imagine stepping outside at noon and needing a torch to see. The air smells of smoke. Snow starts falling in places that have never seen it, and it's grey with ash.
Without sunlight, plants can't make food. Studies suggest photosynthesis almost stopped for a year or more.
Then the cold arrives. Some climate models suggest the planet's average temperature dropped by more than twenty degrees Celsius, and stayed below freezing for years.
Tropical forests freeze. Rain turns acidic. In the oceans, the plankton die first, then the fish that eat them, then everything that eats the fish. The whole food chain collapses from the bottom up.
Now think about us.
Our farms need sunlight just like the forests do. One cold, dark year means no harvest almost anywhere on Earth. And most countries only keep a few months of food in storage.
So the people who make it through the first day face a much harder question.
What do eight billion people eat when the Sun disappears?
Researchers who study global catastrophes have looked at this exact problem. Their answers sound strange, but they work. Mushrooms grown on dead wood. Greenhouses heated by power plants. Even sugar made from leaves and sawdust.
Survival would depend on how fast we could switch the whole world to food that doesn't need the Sun.
But if you could fast forward a few years, you'd see something unexpected.
Life doesn't end. It changes hands.
