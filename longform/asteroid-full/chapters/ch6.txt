First, the good news. Asteroids this big are incredibly rare. A rock ten kilometres wide hits Earth roughly once every hundred million years.
They're also the easiest to find. NASA says it has already found more than ninety percent of the near Earth asteroids bigger than one kilometre, and none of them are on course to hit us for at least the next century.
The real danger is the smaller ones. A rock a few hundred metres wide wouldn't end the world, but it could wipe out a city, or an entire region. And fewer than half of those have been found.
That's why the real weapon isn't a missile. It's time.
If we spot an asteroid years or decades ahead, we don't need to blow it up. We just need to nudge it. A push of just one centimetre per second, given ten years early, adds up to thousands of kilometres by the time it reaches us. Enough to make it miss.
There are a few ways to give that push. Crash a spacecraft into it. Park a heavy spacecraft beside it and let gravity tug it off course, very slowly. Or, for a big one with little time, set off a nuclear device nearby, to blast vapour off one side and shove it away.
In twenty twenty two, NASA tested exactly that.
A spacecraft called DART flew eleven million kilometres and slammed into a small asteroid moon called Dimorphos at about six kilometres per second.
It worked. The impact changed the moon's orbit by thirty two minutes. For the first time ever, humans changed the path of a natural object in space.
And Europe's Hera spacecraft is on its way there right now, to study the crash site up close.
Meanwhile, the hunt is getting faster. The new Vera Rubin Observatory in Chile is scanning the whole southern sky every few nights. And NASA is building a space telescope called NEO Surveyor, made for one job: finding dangerous asteroids before they find us.
The system is already being tested for real. In early twenty twenty five, a newly found asteroid called twenty twenty four Y R four briefly had about a three percent chance of hitting Earth in twenty thirty two. Telescopes around the world tracked it, and within weeks, the threat was ruled out.

An impact has already been ruled out, so instead of a threat, it's a free science lesson, and NASA is sending a spacecraft to meet it.
