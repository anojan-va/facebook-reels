Chapter five. The world it leaves behind.
Slowly, the sky clears. The sulfur rains out within a few years. The dust settles. Sunlight returns.
But the climate swings the other way. All the carbon dioxide released by the impact and the fires traps heat. One study found that after the cold, temperatures rose by about five degrees, and stayed high for around a hundred thousand years.
Sixty six million years ago, when this really happened, about three quarters of all species on Earth disappeared.
Not just the dinosaurs. The giant sea reptiles. The flying pterosaurs. The ammonites, whose shells had filled the oceans for hundreds of millions of years.
So who survived?
The small ones. Animals that could burrow, hide, eat seeds, or live on dead things. Turtles. Crocodiles. Frogs. Birds, the only dinosaurs that made it. And small, furry mammals, most of them tiny enough to hide in a hole in the ground.
With the giants gone, those mammals got their chance. Over the next ten million years they spread into every empty corner of the planet, and grew into whales, horses, elephants, and eventually, primates.
The plants came back too. In rock layers just above the impact, fossil pollen suddenly gives way to fern spores. Ferns were the first plants to cover the burned land.
In a strange way, we're only here because an asteroid cleared the stage.
But this time, the giants are us.
We're big, we need a lot of food, and our whole civilisation depends on a stable climate. A world after impact would have no global trade, no internet, and no supermarkets.
What it would have is what we've saved. Libraries. Survivors with skills. And places like the Global Seed Vault in Norway, buried inside a frozen mountain, holding more than a million samples of the world's crops.
The world it leaves behind is colder, darker and emptier, and then, over thousands of years, green again.
